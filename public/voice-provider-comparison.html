Sarvam vs Cartesia — where each one wins, where each one breaks

A grounded comparison of the two voice stacks already wired into this codebase: what each does well, where each fails for Indic and code-mixed workloads, and what to change before V1.

Author Bhavya · PM portfolio research Date 12 May 2026 Scope TTS · ASR · Latency · Edge cases · Cost Status Pre-bakeoff draft

TL;DR — five things to internalize

  • Sarvam wins on Indic-native naturalness and ASR; Cartesia wins on raw streaming latency and emotional range — and they are not substitutable for Indic-first products without trade-offs.
  • You're benchmarking outdated models. The code runs bulbul:v2 and Cartesia sonic-2 — Bulbul V3 (Feb 2026) and Sonic-3.5 (May 2026) are the current generation. Any bakeoff result on v2/Sonic-2 will under-represent both.
  • Cartesia's ASR (Ink-Whisper) does not publicly enumerate Indic language support. A full-stack Cartesia build for an Indic product likely needs Sarvam Saaras anyway — which collapses the "single provider" thesis.
  • Your pipeline silently penalizes Cartesia. A 1500-char hard cap (Sarvam's limit) is applied to both, and ASR locale is en for Cartesia vs en-IN for Sarvam — apples-to-oranges before audio ever plays.
  • For Mockingbird specifically (PM interview practice, primarily English with Indian-candidate audience, 30-60 min sessions): the contest is closer than the headline numbers suggest. Sarvam's Indian-English voices and long-form stability give it the edge on cultural resonance; Cartesia's latency and expressiveness make it the better fit for fast, conversational follow-ups.

01What's actually wired up today

Before grading anything, here's what the codebase is really doing. Both providers are integrated as stateless edge-runtime proxies in /app/api/voice/, with the client picking provider per request via an X-Voice-Provider header (defaulting to Sarvam).

Sarvam primary · default

  • TTS: Bulbul v2 · speaker anushka (female, Indian-English)
  • ASR: Saaras v3 · language en-IN
  • Sample rate (TTS): 22,050 Hz · WAV 16-bit PCM
  • Auth: api-subscription-key header, pass-through (candidate-owned)
  • Text cap: 1,500 chars (Sarvam Bulbul limit, enforced as the global cap)
  • Source: app/api/voice/synthesize/route.ts:78–135

Cartesia secondary · opt-in via header

  • TTS: Sonic 2 · voice Kiefer (male, English voice-agent persona, UUID 228fca29…)
  • ASR: ink-whisper · language en (no India locale)
  • Sample rate (TTS): 24,000 Hz · WAV 16-bit PCM
  • Auth: Bearer + required cartesia-version: 2025-04-16
  • Response shape: single binary blob, wrapped server-side to match Sarvam's chunked-array shape
  • Source: app/api/voice/synthesize/route.ts:137–197
Reading this honestly: the current setup compares a Sarvam female en-IN voice against a Cartesia male English-accent voice, both throttled to Sarvam's text cap, with Sarvam asked to recognize Indian-English and Cartesia asked to recognize generic English. Any "user prefers X" finding from this configuration is partly a finding about this configuration, not the providers in the abstract.

03What's working — what's not

Sarvam — what's working

  • The Indic substrate is real. Bulbul V3's gains aren't from an English base model getting Hindi adapters bolted on — it's trained on Indian data from scratch. You hear it in the schwa handling and prosody, not just in spec tables.
  • Saaras V3's codemix output mode is uniquely useful for any product that downstream processes the transcript (e.g., your interview transcript display or LLM prompt). Native script tokens stay native; Latin tokens stay Latin. No transliteration tax.
  • Voice-quality on telephony (8kHz) beats Cartesia in the Josh Talks blind study across all 11 Indic languages — relevant if Mockingbird ever ships a phone-call interview mode for low-bandwidth users.
  • Procurement story. For a government-scheme product, on-prem + sovereign compute is a checkbox you literally cannot replicate with Cartesia.

Sarvam — what's not working

  • Latency opacity. No published, reproducible TTS TTFB number. You're trusting sales claims until you measure it yourself.
  • You're on v2. Bulbul v3 dropped Feb 2026. The current code targets the older generation, which means every "Sarvam is fine" or "Sarvam is rough" judgment in your dev loop is on a model that's been superseded.
  • Hardcoded locale. en-IN for ASR and anushka for TTS — no runtime language detection, no per-user voice preference, no fallback when ASR detects (say) Hindi but the route asked for en-IN.
  • No retry / fallback. A Sarvam 5xx is a session-killer right now — no Cartesia failover, no exponential backoff in the edge route.

Cartesia — what's working

  • Latency is genuinely best-in-class for streaming. The SSM architecture is a structural advantage, not a marketing claim. For interrupt-tolerant conversational UX, this matters.
  • Voice library polish. Voice cloning, named-character voices, emotional inflection ("AI laughter"). For an interview-coach experience that wants personality, Cartesia gives you more knobs.
  • Developer experience. Docs, SDKs, and the response model are cleaner than Sarvam's. Onboarding cost is lower.
  • English-first quality. For the PM-interview-prep flow where the transcript will be primarily English with technical jargon, Cartesia produces fluent, broadcast-quality output.

Cartesia — what's not working

  • The Indic story is reactive. India page exists, Indic voices exist, but the underlying model treats Indic as "another locale" rather than native. The number-pronunciation MOS regression (3.45 vs 4.41 in vendor benchmarks) is a tell.
  • Ink-Whisper has an Indic ASR coverage gap. If your transcript needs to capture "मेरा नाम भव्य है" or "PMAY ka kya status hai", you're going to fall back to Sarvam, AssemblyAI, or Deepgram anyway.
  • Pricing model friction. Credits-per-second is hard to forecast against character-based product metrics; the per-character equivalent ends up ~2.5x Sarvam.
  • India compliance posture is partner-mediated, not native. Fine for SaaS, awkward for government.

04Edge cases worth stress-testing

These are the inputs that separate "sounds good in a demo" from "doesn't embarrass us in production." Each row is a single test prompt and the predicted-behavior delta between the two providers. Run these against both before signing off.

  • Schwa-final Hindi nouns"रमेश", "नमस्ते"
    SarvamLLM-based prosody should drop the final schwa correctly (/nəmsteː/ not /nəməsteː/). No public benchmark, but architecturally sound.
    CartesiaHigher risk of inserted schwa or English-cadence stress. Watch for "Ra-MESH-uh" failure mode.
  • Code-mix, English-dominant"Aaj meeting hai at 3pm with the team"
    SarvamSingle-pass should keep both registers; risk: Hindi tokens may sound under-articulated when surrounded by English.
    CartesiaLikely strongest case — English-accent Hindi tokens may even sound natural. The flow advantage is real here.
  • Code-mix, Hindi-dominant"मेरा doctor 5 बजे available है"
    SarvamStrong — Hindi prosody preserved, English tokens pronounced in Indian-English register.
    CartesiaLikely weakest case — English voice "switches off" English fluency to attempt Hindi, then back. Audible seam.
  • PM jargon & product names"north-star metric", "RICE prioritization", "Figma", "Razorpay", "Swiggy"
    SarvamIndian-English voices pronounce Indian company names naturally ("Swiggy", "Razorpay"). Acronyms like "RICE", "MVP", "PMF" are an open question.
    CartesiaLikely stronger on English-origin PM jargon ("OKR", "north-star"); risk of mispronouncing Indian brand names common in PM interview contexts.
  • Numbers, dates, currency"₹1,25,000 तक", "15 अगस्त 2025"
    SarvamDesigned for Indian numbering ("lakh"); should read "एक लाख पच्चीस हज़ार" correctly.
    CartesiaDocumented MOS drop on numeric sentences. May read "1,25,000" as "one hundred twenty-five thousand" — wrong register.
  • Long-form interviewer turnsA 90-second case prompt or scenario setup
    Sarvam1500-char cap forces client-side chunking. Stability claimed; cross-chunk prosody continuity is the risk that breaks "interviewer feels real."
    CartesiaSame 1500-char cap is applied in your pipeline (artificial). Native Cartesia limit is higher — you're under-using it for the exact case where Cartesia might shine (45s+ smooth narration).
  • Network drop mid-utteranceWi-Fi flap on a tier-2-city user's phone
    SarvamNo server-side retry in route handler. Client state machine handles network-drop, but a degraded response will surface the failure to the user.
    CartesiaSame — single binary blob makes partial-stream recovery harder. Consider buffered playback.
  • Provider 5xxUpstream outage
    SarvamReturns 502 to client; no fallback to Cartesia configured.
    CartesiaSymmetric — same failure mode. The dual-provider integration exists but isn't used as a failover today.

05Recommendations for the bakeoff

1
Upgrade both models before benchmarking. Move Sarvam from bulbul:v2 → v3, and Cartesia from sonic-2 → sonic-3.5. Any data collected on the current versions is already historically biased — and given Bulbul V3's reported jump on telephony, the gap to Cartesia is likely larger than the v2 codebase suggests.
2
Make the comparison apples-to-apples. Match voice gender / persona between providers (Sarvam arjun ↔ Cartesia Kiefer, or pick comparable female voices). Set Cartesia ASR locale to hi-IN or whatever fits — or accept that Ink-Whisper is out and use Saaras for both. Lift the 1500-char cap for Cartesia in synthesize/route.ts:70 and measure long-form separately.
3
Measure TTFB yourself. Neither provider publishes a reproducible India-region TTFB number. Add a 30-line latency-probe script (tests/voice-latency.spec.ts) hitting both providers from Vercel edge for the same prompt, 100 trials each, at three text lengths (50 / 250 / 1000 chars). This is your honest data.
4
Run a small PM-candidate listener panel. 5 target users (PM candidates, mix of Indian and international backgrounds) × 8 prompts (the edge-case list above) × 2 providers = 80 ratings. Pairwise A/B, ±3 scale, with one open question: "would you do a 30-minute mock interview with this voice?" This is the cheapest credible quality signal you can produce, and the "would you sit through 30 min" question is the only one that maps to the actual product.
5
Decide the architectural question, not just the model question. The real choice isn't "which model sounds better" — it's "does the product split TTS and ASR providers?" If yes, you keep Saaras + can swap TTS by region/persona/cost. If no, Sarvam is the safer default for an Indic-first product. Write this down in the V1 plan before bakeoff so the data settles a defined question.
6
Build the fallback you don't have. Right now a Sarvam 502 ends the session. The two-provider integration is most of the way there — wire one as automatic failover with a 2.5s timeout in the edge route, then surface the fact ("switched to backup voice") in the UI. Costs ~30 lines and demos resilience in a portfolio review.

06Verdict for Mockingbird

For a voice-based PM interview practice app, primarily English with Indian-candidate audience, where the interviewer voice has to feel credible enough that a candidate will sit through a 30–60 minute session and want to come back:

Primary: Sarvam Bulbul V3 (Indian-English voice). The cultural-resonance argument is product-defining for Mockingbird — an Indian PM candidate hearing a subtly Indian interviewer voice feels different from a generic American voice, and that feeling determines retention. Saaras V3 for ASR is also the right call: PM candidates code-switch into Hindi when they're nervous, and only Saaras handles that without dropping tokens. Secondary: Cartesia Sonic-3.5 as an opt-in "American interviewer" voice (FAANG-style mock prep is a real use case), and as the latency-optimized choice when conversational interruption-tolerance matters more than accent.

The reason to also run Cartesia is not that you might switch — it's that giving users a voice picker ("Indian interviewer" / "American interviewer") is a tiny feature with disproportionate signal: it tells candidates the product understands their context, generates two natural marketing screenshots, and gives you instrumented dual-provider latency / cost data for free. Cost: about 30 lines of code on top of what's already integrated.
